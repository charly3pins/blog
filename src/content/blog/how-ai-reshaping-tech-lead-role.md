The conversation around AI and engineering usually lands in two camps: *it will replace developers*, or *it's just a fancy autocomplete*. Both miss the point, especially if you're a tech lead.

What I'm actually seeing is subtler — and more interesting.

## The bottleneck has moved

For most of my career, the bottleneck in a team was **writing code**. Reviews, PRs, implementation cycles. That's still true, but less so. With AI-assisted coding, a competent engineer can produce working code significantly faster than before.

The new bottleneck is **knowing what to build and why**.

This is where the tech lead role becomes more important, not less. Someone has to translate ambiguous product requirements into system design decisions. Someone has to push back when the proposed solution will create technical debt you'll spend two years paying off. Someone has to spot the gap between what the AI-generated code *does* and what the system *needs*.

That's judgment. You can't autocomplete judgment.

## What's actually changing in my day to day

A few things I've noticed:

**Code reviews are different.** I'm no longer reviewing line-by-line as much. The AI catches obvious issues. I'm reviewing architecture, edge cases, and whether the abstraction makes sense for where this system will be in 12 months.

**Prototyping is faster.** A spike that used to take two days takes half a day. This is genuinely good — it means we can validate assumptions earlier before committing to an approach.

**The gap between senior and junior has changed shape.** Juniors can produce more output. But seniors are still the ones who know when *not* to ship something, or when the "working" solution is wrong for reasons that won't be obvious for six months.

## The skill that matters more now

If I had to pick one: **the ability to define the problem precisely**.

Vague prompts produce vague code. Vague requirements produce systems that technically work but solve the wrong thing. The engineers who will do well in this era are the ones who can think with clarity about what a system actually needs to do — before touching a keyboard.

That's always been important. It's just more exposed now.

---

Still figuring out what this means long term. But I'd rather be paying attention to the shift than pretending it isn't happening.
